Selected work

Egg carrying glider, design and flight test

A first year design and build project, and a brief that is easy to state and hard to pass: design an aircraft, fly it, and land it with the payload still intact. The payload was an egg. The whole airframe was designed in SolidWorks before anything was cut.

The aircraft flew. It then flipped in a strong crosswind and the motor detached from its mount. The cause was mine. The mounting hole had been drilled too shallow, so the fixing had far less thread engagement than the gust case demanded. It held on the ground and it held through the first part of the flight, which is exactly why nobody caught it.

The egg survived. So against the brief we passed, and against any honest reading of the flight we did not. Both of those are true and I would rather say both than pick the flattering one.

What I took from it: a joint that carries the motor deserves a calculation, not a feel. Static load on the bench tells you almost nothing about a gust, and the failure was in the fastener, not the structure around it.

Land speed record braking analysis

A first year analysis exercise, leading a team of 6. We worked from Bloodhound thrust vector and velocity data in Excel to calculate the braking point a record attempt would need.

The arithmetic was not the hard part. The answer is only as good as what you assume the vehicle is doing at the moment braking starts, and every one of those assumptions is a choice somebody has to stand behind in the write up.

Manufacturing pitch: 1,000 gliders on £10,000

Led a team of 6 to plan production of a thousand gliders against a ten thousand pound investment. Unit cost model, marketing strategy and a quality optimisation plan, pitched and marked.

The cost model is where it stopped being an exercise. Every quality step you add is a cost you have to defend, and the plan only holds together if you can say what each check actually buys you at the end of the line.

02

Industry experience

Summer internship, Thales UK, Crawley

Six weeks over the summer of 2026, shadowing engineers on live projects. A lot of it was watching and asking questions, which is the honest description of an internship at this stage, but it was the first time I saw engineering run as a business rather than as coursework.

What I was following was how a requirement gets defined, allocated to a part of the system, and then verified, and what the documentation around all of that has to look like before anyone will sign it off. I also saw the MBSE toolchains in use on site, Capella and DOORS, closely enough to understand what they are for rather than to claim I can drive them.

What I took from it: the engineering is only half of the job. The other half is the record, written so that somebody who was not in the room can check the work years later.
